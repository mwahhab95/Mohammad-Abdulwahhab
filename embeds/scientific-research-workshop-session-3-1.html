1. The Art of Scientific Storytelling

Transforming Data into a Compelling Narrative

Why Storytelling Matters?

As pharmacists, we are trained to be precise, objective, and data-driven. We present facts. However, for those facts to have a real-world impact—whether on a fellow clinician, a patient, or a research committee—they must be presented within a narrative. A story provides context, creates an emotional connection, and makes complex information digestible and memorable. The simplest and most powerful narrative structure for science is the "And, But, Therefore" (ABT) framework.

The "And, But, Therefore" (ABT) Framework

The ABT framework is the backbone of almost every story ever told. It consists of three parts that create a logical and engaging flow:

AND

This is the context or the established knowledge. It's the world as we know it, a statement of agreement that sets the stage.

BUT

This is the crucial turning point. It introduces the problem, the contradiction, or the knowledge gap that creates tension.

THEREFORE

This is the solution or the new insight. It resolves the tension and presents your work as the logical conclusion.

Examples in Action

Clinical Pharmacy Example:

"Current guidelines for managing hypertension in diabetic patients recommend ACE inhibitors as a first-line therapy, AND they are widely prescribed due to their proven efficacy in reducing cardiovascular events. BUT, a significant portion of our patient population reports a persistent dry cough, leading to poor adherence and discontinuation of this life-saving medication. THEREFORE, our study evaluated the comparative adherence and blood pressure control between ACE inhibitors and ARBs, demonstrating that an alternative first-line strategy could significantly improve patient outcomes."

Pharmaceutical Research Example:

"Compound XYZ has shown incredible potency against cancer cells in vitro, AND its mechanism of action is well-understood. BUT, its poor aqueous solubility makes it impossible to formulate into an effective intravenous drug, halting its clinical development. THEREFORE, we developed a novel nanoparticle delivery system that encapsulates Compound XYZ, dramatically increasing its solubility and bioavailability, paving the way for in-vivo testing."

Training: Frame Your Own Story

Now, it's your turn. Think about your current project, presentation, or paper. Use the template below to distill your complex work into a single, powerful ABT statement. This will become the core message of your communication.

For years, we have known that... [Your AND statement establishing common ground].

BUT, the major challenge/problem/gap in knowledge is... [Your BUT statement introducing the conflict].

THEREFORE, my research/project aims to... [Your THEREFORE statement presenting the resolution].

2. Mastering the Podium

Crafting and Delivering Unforgettable Oral Presentations

The Golden Rule: 12/12/24

Conference presentations are strictly timed. A simple rule to ensure you are clear, concise, and respectful of time is the 12 Slides / 12 Minutes / 24+ Point Font rule. Let's break it down.

  • 12 Slides: This forces you to focus on your core message. A typical structure is: Title (1), Introduction/ABT (1), Methods (2), Results (4-5), Conclusion (1), Future Work (1), Acknowledgements (1), Q&A (1). It prevents you from overwhelming the audience with data.
  • 12 Minutes: This standard conference slot (often 12 min talk + 3 min Q&A) requires discipline. Aim for about one minute per slide. Rehearsing your talk is not optional—it is essential to ensure you finish on time without rushing.
  • 24+ Point Font: Your slides must be readable from the back of a large room. Avoid tiny fonts and dense paragraphs. Use keywords, phrases, and visuals. Your slides are a visual aid, not a script.

Techniques for a Powerful Delivery

1. Practice for Confidence

  • Rehearse your talk out loud multiple times.
  • Time yourself to ensure you fit the schedule.
  • Record yourself to analyze your pacing and body language.
  • Present to colleagues or friends for valuable feedback.

2. Simplify for Clarity

  • Avoid or clearly explain any necessary jargon.
  • Follow the "one idea per slide" principle to maintain focus.
  • Use high-quality graphs and images to show, not just tell.
  • Ensure each slide has one single, memorable takeaway message.

3. Engage with the Audience

  • Make eye contact with different sections of the audience.
  • Vary your vocal tone, pace, and volume to maintain interest.
  • Use purposeful gestures to emphasize key points.
  • Move around the stage to command attention.

3. Designing an Award-Winning Poster

Creating a Visual Magnet for Your Research

Posters as Conversations

A poster is not a research paper taped to a board. It's a visual abstract designed to do one thing: attract people and start a conversation. Your goal is to create a design that is so clear and compelling that a viewer can understand your main findings in under 3 minutes, even from a distance. Think of it as an advertisement for your research.

Core Principles of Poster Design

1. Title & Authors

Your title should be a punchy statement and the largest text on the poster. List authors and their affiliations clearly underneath.

2. The 2-Meter Rule

Can someone grasp the main point from 2 meters away? The title, key figures, and main takeaway must be visible from a distance to draw people in.

3. Logical Flow

Guide the reader's eye with columns (usually 3 or 4). The path should be obvious and intuitive: top-to-bottom, left-to-right.

4. Prioritize Visuals

Show, don't tell. Graphs, charts, and high-quality images should dominate the space. A single, powerful central figure is highly effective.

5. Embrace White Space

Don't cram every inch with content. Empty space (or "negative space") makes your poster look less intimidating and helps guide the reader's eye.

6. Colors & Theme

Use a simple, professional color palette (2-3 main colors). Ensure high contrast between text and background for maximum readability.

7. Minimal References

Keep the reference list extremely short (3-5 key citations) in a small font size at the bottom. A QR code can link to a more extensive list.

8. QR Code

A modern essential. Link it to a PDF of your poster, the full paper, or your professional profile. It's a great space-saver for extra details.

Poster Creation Tools & Dimensions

Recommended Software

  • PowerPoint/Google Slides: Accessible and easy to use. Set the slide size to your final poster dimensions from the start.
  • Canva: Great for user-friendly design with many templates.
  • Adobe Illustrator/Inkscape: Professional vector graphics tools that offer maximum control for custom designs.

Common Metric Sizes (cm)

  • Portrait (Vertical):

    • A1: 59.4 cm x 84.1 cm
    • A2: 42.0 cm x 59.4 cm
  • Landscape (Horizontal):
    • A0: 118.9 cm x 84.1 cm
    • A1: 84.1 cm x 59.4 cm
    • Common custom: 120 cm x 90 cm

Training: Your 3-Minute Poster Pitch

When someone stops at your poster, you need a concise, engaging pitch ready. It should take no more than 3 minutes and follow your poster's narrative.

1. The Hook (15 sec): Start with your title and main finding. "Hi, thanks for stopping by. In our work, we found that a new once-daily pill can improve patient adherence by almost 20%."

2. The Walk-through (90 sec): Briefly walk them through your poster, pointing to the key figures. "We know adherence is a big problem [point to intro], so we ran a trial comparing the new pill to the old one [point to methods], and here's what we found [point to main graph]."

3. The "So What?" (15 sec): End with the conclusion and its importance. "This means we have a clear strategy to help patients better control their blood pressure. Do you have any questions?"

4. Mastering the Q&A Session

Turning Questions into Opportunities

The Q&A is Part of the Presentation

Many presenters treat the Q&A session as an afterthought, but it's one of the most important parts of your talk. It's an opportunity to clarify your points, demonstrate the depth of your knowledge, and engage directly with your audience in a scientific dialogue. A well-handled Q&A session can solidify a good presentation into a great one.

The L-R-A Technique: Listen, Rephrase, Answer

A simple, effective technique for handling almost any question is L-R-A.

1. Listen Carefully

Give the questioner your full attention. Don't interrupt. Listen to the entire question, not just the first few words. This shows respect and ensures you understand what is actually being asked.

2. Rephrase for Clarity

This is the most critical step. Briefly rephrase the question back to the asker. This does two things: 1) It confirms you understood the question correctly, and 2) It gives your brain a few extra seconds to formulate a concise answer. Example: "So, if I'm hearing you correctly, you're asking about the potential off-target effects of this drug?"

3. Answer Concisely

Start with a direct answer, then provide a brief explanation. Avoid rambling. A good answer is clear, confident, and directly addresses the rephrased question. Once you've answered, stop talking. It's okay to have a moment of silence.

Handling Difficult Questions

The "I Don't Know" Question

It is perfectly acceptable not to know the answer. Honesty is better than bluffing.

Professional Response: "That's an excellent question. Our study wasn't designed to look at that specific outcome, but it's an important area for future research. I'd be happy to discuss it with you afterwards."

The Hostile or Challenging Question

Stay calm and professional. Do not get defensive. Address the scientific merit of the question, not the tone.

Professional Response: "I appreciate that perspective, thank you. While our data points towards this conclusion, I agree that exploring alternative interpretations is valuable. The methodology we used is standard for this type of study, but we can certainly discuss the limitations."

The Vague or Multi-Part Question

Use your rephrasing power to break it down or ask for clarification.

Professional Response: "That's a great point with a few parts to it. To make sure I answer what you're most interested in, could we start with the first part about the patient selection criteria?"

Training: Anticipate and Prepare

The best way to master the Q&A is to prepare for it. As you rehearse your talk, think about the questions you might be asked. What are the weakest points in your argument? What are the most controversial findings? What might be confusing?

Action Item: Write down the three toughest questions you could possibly receive about your presentation. Then, practice answering each one using the Listen-Rephrase-Answer technique. This exercise alone will dramatically boost your confidence.
